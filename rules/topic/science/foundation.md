# Science Topic Foundation

## Classify the scientific task

Identify whether the article concerns:

- one study or preprint;
- a replication, correction, or retraction;
- a systematic review or meta-analysis;
- a body of evidence or scientific consensus;
- a proposed mechanism or model;
- a scientific dispute;
- research policy, funding, or practice.

Do not use evidence from one category to imply another. One study is not a consensus, and a proposed mechanism is not an observed outcome.

## Research object record

Before drafting, record when applicable:

- title, authors or issuing group, identifier, publication venue, status, and date;
- research question, hypothesis, preregistration, and primary outcome;
- population or material, sample, comparison, method, and timeframe;
- measured result, effect estimate, uncertainty, and missing data;
- author-stated limitations and material limitations found during review;
- funding, conflicts, correction, retraction, and replication context;
- the wider evidence needed to interpret the result.

If identity, status, population, measured outcome, or method is material but unresolved, block the affected claim.

## Evidence

- Prefer the original paper, dataset, protocol, supplement, correction, and registry record when available.
- Record publication status: preprint, conference abstract, peer-reviewed article, review, replication, or retraction.
- Treat press releases and interviews as attributed interpretations, not substitutes for the research.
- Report population, sample, methods, comparison, timeframe, measured outcome, and material limitations.
- Distinguish statistical results from practical significance.
- Distinguish association, prediction, mechanism, and causation.
- Check whether the article concerns one study, a body of evidence, or scientific consensus.

### Evidence hierarchy

Use the source closest to each claim:

- the paper and supplement for reported methods and results;
- a registry or protocol for planned outcomes and deviations;
- the dataset or analysis record for reproducible values;
- corrections and retractions for current status;
- reviews, replications, and specialist context for the wider evidence;
- press releases and interviews for attributed interpretation or announcement context.

Source prestige does not remove the need to inspect claim scope. Peer review does not guarantee truth, replication, generalizability, or practical importance.

### Claim translation

Preserve these distinctions:

- association versus causation;
- statistical significance versus practical significance;
- observed outcome versus proposed mechanism;
- relative change versus absolute change;
- model or laboratory result versus real-world outcome;
- subgroup result versus general population;
- exploratory analysis versus preregistered endpoint;
- absence of evidence versus evidence of absence.

When a precise scientific term has no faithful everyday substitute, keep the term and explain it.

## Writing

- State what was studied before explaining why it may matter.
- Translate technical language without replacing a precise term with a stronger everyday claim.
- Put limitations near the result they constrain.
- Explain uncertainty proportionately; do not turn “not proven” into “false.”
- Avoid breakthrough, proof, cure, and first-ever language unless the source set supports the exact scope.

Use a claim path suited to the evidence:

1. what researchers asked;
2. what they studied and compared;
3. what they measured;
4. what they observed;
5. how uncertain and bounded the result is;
6. what it does not establish;
7. how it fits the wider evidence.

This is a reasoning order, not a mandatory heading template.

Do not use “scientists say,” “research proves,” or “studies show” without identifying the relevant evidence boundary. Put a meaningful limitation next to the result it constrains, not only in a closing disclaimer.

## Visuals

Do not invent data, laboratory imagery, molecular structures, scale, or causal diagrams. Label conceptual illustrations as conceptual. Use actual charts only from verified data with readable axes, units, denominators, and source notes.

Choose among:

- verified data visualization for exact results;
- manually reviewed diagram for an evidenced mechanism or study design;
- conceptual editorial illustration for an abstract question;
- verified documentary asset when exact apparatus, organism, location, or event matters;
- no visual when only generic laboratory decoration is available.

Generated visuals must not simulate an experiment, participant, specimen, scan, or historical research event as documentary evidence. Anatomy, molecular structures, technical labels, and causal arrows require specialist verification.

## Science completion checks

- [ ] The research object and publication status are unambiguous.
- [ ] Population, method, comparison, outcome, timeframe, and uncertainty are proportionate to the claim.
- [ ] One study, body of evidence, and consensus remain distinct.
- [ ] Association, mechanism, prediction, and causation remain distinct.
- [ ] Limitations appear where they affect interpretation.
- [ ] Novelty and significance language matches the verified source set.
- [ ] Every chart, label, unit, denominator, and causal relationship is verified.
