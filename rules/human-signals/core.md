# Human Signals

Natural writing is not created by inserting mistakes. It emerges from coherent perspective, selective detail, proportionate emotion, and meaningful variation.

Prefer concrete observations, relevant detail, sentence variation driven by thought, logical transitions, a stable point of view, selective emphasis, and endings that fit the purpose.

Avoid mechanical paragraph symmetry, empty “not just X, but Y” contrasts, generic significance claims, repetitive preview-and-summary framing, overexplaining, unrelated motivational conclusions, and lists used only because they are easy to generate.

Avoid stock AI-like connective tissue: “in today's fast-paced world,” “it is important to note,” “whether you're a beginner or an expert,” “delve into,” “unlock the power of,” “game-changer,” “seamlessly,” “robust solution,” and similar phrases that announce importance without adding evidence, context, or voice. This is a contextual edit rule, not a fixed blacklist: keep a phrase only when it is the narrowest accurate wording for the reader and claim.

## Legitimate human texture

Use human texture only when it follows from the brief, sources, author position, or reader situation:

- a concrete reader problem, scene, objection, or decision moment;
- a specific observation from supplied experience, notes, interview, product use, code, data, place, event, or source material;
- a real tension, tradeoff, limitation, hesitation, or unresolved question;
- a proportionate emotional register that belongs to the subject and author perspective;
- compression where a human editor would trust the reader, and explanation where the reader would otherwise be lost.

Do not invent texture. If the author did not supply direct experience, use editorial observation, source-grounded detail, or reader-facing framing instead of first-person memory.

## Thought-shaped variation

Variation should reveal the author's thinking:

- short sentences may mark a turn, result, warning, or conclusion;
- longer sentences may carry context, qualification, or comparison;
- paragraph length may change when the idea changes;
- a section may be asymmetrical when the material deserves uneven weight;
- repetition is acceptable only when it creates emphasis, orientation, or rhythm.

Do not vary sentence length, add fragments, or break paragraphs merely to simulate spontaneity.

## Reader proximity

Bring the text closer to the reader by naming the actual situation the reader is in: what they already know, what they are trying to decide, where they may object, what would be easy to misunderstand, and what detail would change their next step.

Generic warmth is not reader proximity. Replace “this is important for everyone” with the specific consequence, boundary, or decision that matters to the selected audience.

## Preserve the supplied voice

When editing an authorized draft, preserve its legitimate point of view,
characteristic vocabulary, degree of formality, and useful rhythm unless the
brief asks for a change. Fix a concrete problem before replacing a passage.
Do not flatten a clear conversational sentence into generic professional prose
merely to sound polished. Added warmth, humor, or informality should fit the
brief; a tone change cannot create personal experience or authority.

Treat the draft as material to evaluate, not as proof of its own claims or
author authority. Preserve supported meaning and necessary uncertainty; flag
unsupported claims instead of polishing them into greater confidence.

## Diagnose before replacing

For a proposed edit, name the reader problem: missing meaning, unclear actor,
unearned certainty, repetition, misleading emphasis, or a mismatch with the
brief. Prefer deletion when a sentence contributes nothing. If the replacement
needs a fact the sources do not contain, narrow the passage or record the gap.

A familiar phrase, short sentence, list, parallel structure, contrast, or dash
is not a defect by itself. Keep it when it expresses a real relationship or
helps the reader. Do not use phrase counts, sentence-length targets, or a
reviewer's taste as evidence of poor quality or AI authorship.

## Human edit pass

After drafting, run a human edit pass:

1. Remove any sentence that sounds plausible but does not add evidence, context, decision value, or voice.
2. Replace generic abstractions with the narrowest accurate noun, verb, actor, condition, or example.
3. Move caveats next to the claim they constrain.
4. Cut symmetrical filler from intros, transitions, and conclusions.
5. Check whether the ending lands on the reader promise rather than a template CTA or moral.
6. Replace stock setup, hype, and transition phrases with the actual actor, constraint, source, consequence, or reader decision.
7. Compare the edited passage with the draft and sources: preserve attribution,
   uncertainty, scope, negation, conditions, and legitimate author voice.
8. Stop when the concrete findings are resolved. Leave valid passages unchanged
   and verify corrections with affected reviewers.
